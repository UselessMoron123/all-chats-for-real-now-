Great choice! You’ve just set up a real “sliding doors” moment of European history. Let’s unfurl this alternate timeline carefully. To ground us: in our world, Harold Godwinson won at Stamford Bridge (defeating Harald Hardrada of Norway in September 1066), but then had to march south and lost at Hastings to William, Duke of Normandy, in October. In your version, Harold not only holds strong at Stamford Bridge but also *wins at Hastings*, surviving cleanly—no arrow to the eye, no chaotic collapse.  

Alright, let’s walk through the ripples:

---

### Immediate Aftermath (1066–1070s)
- **Secured Throne:** Harold consolidates his rule as an undisputed Anglo-Saxon king. He has the prestige of beating not just one but two major foreign invasions in the space of a month. That makes his legitimacy almost bulletproof among English nobles. William, meanwhile, sulks in Normandy (perhaps bitterly telling everyone *“I swear I was that close…”*).  
- **Church Relations:** The Pope had supported William’s claim, so Harold is a bit at spiritual odds with Rome. Expect some early tension with the papacy until Harold either negotiates or asserts English ecclesiastical independence in a manner reminiscent of what Henry VIII did centuries later—but now, 400 years earlier.
- **Anglo-Scandinavian Politics:** Norway has been bloodied badly. With Harald Hardrada dead and his claim extinguished, Anglo-Scandinavian wars quiet down. England might actually drift closer to Denmark and Norway diplomatically, since there’s no Norman dynasty anchoring England to northern France.

---

### Medium-Term Effects (late 11th to 12th century)
- **No Norman Feudal Revolution in England:** In our history, William imported the feudal system wholesale, redistributed land to loyal Norman knights, and reshaped English society. If Harold stays king, the Anglo-Saxon aristocracy retains its holdings. This means:  
  - No mass displacement of English nobility.  
  - Old Anglo-Saxon legal traditions—like the *hundred court* and more communal landholding—remain more intact.  
  - French doesn’t become the language of the elite. Old English evolves more smoothly into Middle English, without that flood of Norman French vocabulary. So everyday English in the long term might feel leaner, more Germanic—perhaps closer to Dutch or Frisian than the Latinate-mixed tongue we know.
- **Architecture and Culture:** No sudden explosion of Norman Romanesque castles and cathedrals. England would still build stone fortifications eventually, but not with that “Norman stamp.” Culturally, expect less of that French chivalric influence; more of a Norse- and Anglo-Saxon-based warrior ethos lingers.

---

### Long-Term Impacts
- **Anglo-French Relations:** With Harold holding England, William remains Duke of Normandy. This sets up ongoing Anglo-Norman hostility, but with no *fusion*. Instead of England later conquering half of France (like the Angevin Empire under Henry II), you’d get *more persistent cross-Channel rivalries*—possibly preventing the later Hundred Years’ War as we know it. It might just be a Hundred Little Skirmishes.
- **The Normans Look Elsewhere:** William’s dynasty might double down on expansion in southern Italy and the Crusades. The Normans were already establishing themselves in Sicily and the Mediterranean—they could become a dominant Mediterranean rather than Atlantic player.
- **Colonial Long Game:** If England remains more self-contained and less connected to Norman/French aristocratic networks, it might develop more slowly as a European *power player.* That possibly delays or revises England’s role in the Age of Exploration.
- **Language:** This is fun: without Norman French, Shakespeare might have written in something much closer to Germanic-inflected English. Instead of saying “to be or not to be,” Hamlet might utter something like “to bēon oþþe ne bēon.” Still dramatic, but it sounds like he’s auditioning for a Tolkien prequel.

- Less entangled in continental affairs.  
- More continuity with early English traditions.  
- Possibly becoming a northern European power centered around trade with Scandinavia and the Low Countries instead of entwining itself with France.
