Ah, fantastic catch! That line isn’t from the peasants scene, but from the **Black Knight** duel — and yes, that phrasing *“’Tis but a scratch”* sounds very archaic to our modern ears. Let’s untangle why:  

### 1. The archaic “’tis”
- **“’Tis”** is simply a contraction of *“it is”*.  
- You’ll find it in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, older poetry — basically everything from the 16th–18th centuries.  
- Nobody in modern speech actually says *’tis*, unless they’re being silly, theatrical, or deliberately old‑fashioned.  

### 2. Why Python uses it
- The joke lands because we hear “medieval knight” and expect grand, archaic speech. So the Black Knight obliges, tossing in a line like *“’tis but a scratch”* when his arm has just been cut clean off.  
- Combine that formal old‑sounding phrasing with completely ridiculous understatement (“scratch”…) and the comedy multiplies.  

### 3. The blend
Monty Python constantly mashed:  
- **Archaic forms**: things like *“’tis”*, *“thou art”*, mock‑heroic flourishes.  
- **Modern understatement**: flat, casual reactions like *“oh, it’s just a scratch,” no big deal.*  
- That’s exactly the clash! The words sound lofty, but the content is utterly mundane (or absurd).  

---

So to your point: *yes*. We don’t speak that way now. Even at the time (1970s) it already sounded quaint, which is what made it so ripe for parody. By dropping in just a sprinkle of *’tis* or *thou*, they gave the characters a vaguely “Arthurian” flavor, then undermined it with absurdity.  

That way, it feels medievalish, but it’s never authentic Middle English — more like a costume-party version: just enough “olde timey” sugar dusted on modern humor to trigger the laugh.
