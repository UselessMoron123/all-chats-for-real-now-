**LATE CRETACEOUS — HELL CREEK FLOODPLAIN**
**66,000,000 BCE, give or take a Tuesday**

---

You land in warm mud with a wet *thup*.

You are approximately 2.4 meters of *Saccharum officinarum* — jointed, fibrous, and roughly 14% sucrose by weight. This last fact is about to become the single most important statistic on the planet.

The air is thick and soupy, maybe 30°C, oxygen-rich enough that a dragonfly the size of a dinner plate wobbles past your topmost leaf and keeps going. Around you: ferns, cycads, a stand of magnolias that look almost familiar. Some scrubby, primitive grasses — your extremely distant, extremely unimpressive cousins. They have no idea what you are. Honestly, neither does anything else here.

Because nothing on Earth has ever tasted anything like you.

And something has noticed.

Forty meters off, at the edge of the horsetails, a *Thescelosaurus* — bipedal, about the size of a large pony, beaked, nervy — has stopped mid-chew. Its nostrils flare. It takes one step toward you. Then another.

Above and behind it, unnoticed, the eastern sky has begun to develop a small, bright, entirely new star.

---

You have no legs, no mouth, and no voice. You do have:

- **Nodes** — each one a dormant bud, capable of rooting if it touches soil
- **Sap** — dense, sweet, and to this ecosystem, functionally a narcotic
- **Fiber** — you are tougher than you look
- **Roughly 11 minutes** before that star arrives

What do you do?

**T‑MINUS 11 MINUTES**

---

You do nothing. You do it superbly. Doing nothing is, after all, the core competency of your entire kingdom — 400 million years of standing still and being right about it.

The light in the sky is not a star.

It's low in the southeast, just above the treeline, and in the ninety seconds since you noticed it, it has gone from *pinprick* to *thumbnail* to something that throws your shadow across the mud in a hard, wrong direction — a second sun, rising in the wrong place, at the wrong hour, far too fast.

And it is **completely silent.**

That's the part that would frighten you if you had a nervous system. Light is fast. Sound is not. Whatever made that thing happened a long way from here, and the noise of it is still crossing the continent on foot.

---

**OOC — what the star actually is**

It already happened. About two minutes ago, roughly 3,000 km southeast of you, in the shallow carbonate sea over what will eventually be called the Yucatán, an asteroid somewhere around 10–14 km wide hit the planet at ~20 km/s. It released on the order of 100 million megatons.

What you're seeing isn't the asteroid. It's the **plume** — a column of vaporized rock, seawater and evaporite punched clean out of the atmosphere, tall enough to crest the horizon and shine down on Montana. The impact itself is below the curve of the Earth. You will never see it.

Here's your local schedule:

| Time | Arrival |
|---|---|
| ~T+5 min | Seismic P‑waves. The ground jolts. |
| ~T+11 min | **Ejecta re‑entry begins.** Trillions of molten glass spherules, thrown on ballistic arcs, start falling back through the atmosphere worldwide. Friction turns the entire sky into a broiler element. |
| ~T+2.5 hr | The sound. And the wind behind it. |

The spherule fall is the one that matters. For roughly the next hour, everything standing on the open surface of the Earth cooks. Not burns — *bakes*, from a red-hot sky.

Underground is a different story. **Remember that.**

---

**T‑MINUS 6 MINUTES**

The *Thescelosaurus* has closed the distance. Up close it's shabbier than you expected — mud-caked, one nostril crusted, a healed gash along its flank. It has spent its whole life eating ferns, which taste like wet paper and mild despair.

It lowers its beak to your third internode and bites.

There is a crack like a snapped broom handle.

And then the animal *stops.* Utterly. Its pupils blow wide. Sap — clear, syrupy, fourteen percent sucrose — runs down its jaw and drips into the mud, and for the first and last time in the 165-million-year history of the dinosaurs, a member of that clade experiences **dessert.**

It makes a sound you have no name for.

Then the ground kicks. Hard. Every horsetail in the marsh whips sideways at once, herons that aren't quite herons burst out of the reeds, and the *Thescelosaurus* bolts — with the top third of you clamped in its beak, and the rest of you knocked flat into wet floodplain silt.

Two of your nodes are now pressed into mud. One is still in open air.

---

**T‑MINUS 5 MINUTES**

You're a plant. You cannot run, shout, or decide. But you are not without verbs. Plants do things — slowly, chemically, and with tremendous commitment.

Available to you:

- **Root.** Push from the buried nodes. It's the wrong season, the wrong hemisphere, the wrong *era* — but sugarcane doesn't care, and buried tissue doesn't burn.
- **Hold.** Seal the wound, close your stomata, clamp down on every drop of water in your stalk. Ride it out as one long fibrous thermos.
- **Signal.** Bleed volatiles — the green-leaf compounds every wounded grass screams with. Nothing here speaks your language. Something might still come.
- **Follow.** Do nothing at all, and let the animal carry your severed third away. Wherever it runs, you go. It won't run far. But it might run *down.*

Five minutes. What does the cane do?

**OOC — straight answer, then the options.**

## Will you still see dinosaurs?

**Yes — and for longer than you'd think.** The impact doesn't flick a switch. Here's the real sequence:

| When | What's happening | Dinosaurs?  |
|---|---|---|
| **Hour 1** | Thermal pulse from re-entering ejecta. Anything exposed on open ground cooks. | Mass casualties — but shade, water, burrows, and cloud cover save a lot. |
| **Hours 2–3** | Airblast and sound arrive. Forests flatten. | Survivors are dazed, deafened, wandering. |
| **Days–weeks** | Soot and sulfate aerosols close the sky. Twilight at noon. | **Still very much alive.** Hungry, but alive. |
| **Months–~2 years** | Impact winter. Freezing dark. Photosynthesis collapses. | This is what actually kills them. Big bodies starve first. |
| **After** | The "fern spike" — a real fossil signature. Ferns inherit the bare earth. | Only birds remain. Which are dinosaurs. Forever. |

So the thing that ends the Cretaceous isn't fire — it's **famine in the dark**, and it takes months to years. *T. rex*, *Triceratops*, *Edmontosaurus*, *Ankylosaurus* are all still walking around long after the sky goes out. They're just increasingly desperate.

## Will the setting change completely?

**Yes.** The warm, humming, green floodplain you landed in has about sixty minutes left. It does not come back.

But I'd argue what replaces it is the *better* story for you specifically — because here's the thing I've been setting up:

You are a **disturbance specialist.** Sugarcane's whole evolutionary strategy is colonizing scorched, stripped, churned-up ground by regrowing from buried nodes. Post-impact Earth is, quite literally, your ideal habitat. And in a world where the sun has gone out and every calorie on the continent is running out, you are 2.4 meters of **concentrated sugar** — the single densest source of usable energy in the hemisphere.

A stalk of cane in the Cretaceous is a curiosity. A stalk of cane in the *impact winter* is the most valuable object on the planet, and the starving survivors will find that out.

## So — four ways to run it

1. **Stay on the clock.** Minute by minute through the pulse and the blast. Visceral, short, brutal. You feel the whole thing happen to you.
2. **Skip to the dark.** Jump weeks ahead. Dinosaurs still here — gaunt, cold, and following the smell of sugar. Maximum dinosaur screen time, maximum stakes.
3. **Rewind the clock.** ← *say the word and I'll do this.* You didn't arrive eleven minutes before the end — you arrived **three weeks** before, or three years. You get the full living Hell Creek first: the herds, the river, the *rex*, the pterosaurs. The asteroid becomes act three instead of the opening.
4. **Deep time.** Play as a lineage, not a stalk. Centuries per scene. Watch the survivors become birds around you.

The lush world you landed in is only lost if you want it to be. **Which one?**
