## The Annals of Leinster (CK2 AAR)  
*Chapter I: A Small Crown on a Wet Island*  

The year is 1066, and Ireland is still a story people tell themselves.

Not a kingdom—at least not yet. More like a quilt thrown over a cold body: stitched county to county, each lord tugging at his corner, each priest insisting the pattern was ordained. The sea hems the island in like a gray habit; the wind makes a sermon out of every hillside. In the halls of the petty kings, firelight is the only grandeur that lasts.

Murchad mac Diarmait, Petty King of Leinster, sits under that firelight and listens to men pretend the world is simple.

A steward with ink-stained fingers says the granaries are thin. A marshal with a scar that never quite healed says the levies could be thicker. A chaplain says sin is thicker than both, and asks for funds to correct it. Somewhere beyond the walls, peasants cough into their hands and call it winter.

Murchad nods as though he can nod his way into safety.

He is not old. That is both blessing and threat. Young rulers are like green wood: full of life, slow to burn, but when they catch, they catch loudly.

And there are eyes on Leinster—always eyes. The neighboring petty kings who speak of *peace* like it is a bargain. The Norse in their coastal holds, half-ghosts of old terror, half-merchants with counting boards. Across the sea, the bigger storms: England and Wales stirring, Normandy sharpening its knives.

Ireland feels quiet only because its screams haven’t started yet.

I’m also checking succession—because half of CK2 is ruling, the other half is preventing your realm from being inherited by a drooling cousin in a different dynasty.

---

Murchad rises and walks the perimeter of the hall as if movement itself is governance. The stones of the keep are damp; they keep the memory of rain the way men keep grudges. He stops by a narrow slit window and looks out at the land that is “his.”

Leinster is not a single thing. It is farms and bogs, oaks that refuse to be civilized, roads that turn to mud the moment you look away. It is also names—names that matter more than the soil. A king’s power lives in the mouths of other men. If they stop saying *Petty King of Leinster* with the right kind of respect, the title becomes a joke you can stab.

He returns to the high seat and studies his council.

These men are not friends. Friends are for songs and childhood. Councillors are tools that sometimes speak.

The marshal watches the room like an animal that expects a fight and worries it won’t come. The steward counts silently even when no one asks him to. The spymaster smiles too quickly, a man who cannot help but look guilty even when he tells the truth. The chaplain—God’s representative in a land that has seen too many gods—waits with the patience of someone who believes time ultimately belongs to him.

Murchad lets the silence stretch. He has learned this: men will tell you more to fill quiet than they will to answer questions.

“Who among my neighbors grows restless?” he asks at last.

They glance at each other, each of them deciding whether honesty is safer than usefulness.

*Player thoughts:* Council setup. I’m doing the classic early-game checklist:  
- **Marshal:** train troops in capital.  
- **Steward:** collect taxes in capital.  
- **Chancellor:** fabricate claim on a neighbor (Ossory is tempting, but I’m looking at de jure and easy targets).  
- **Chaplain:** improve relations with the Pope or proselytize depending on need. In Ireland, religion is mostly stable, but papal money can be a lifesaver if I end up in a bad war.  
- **Spymaster:** scheme in capital to discourage plots, unless there’s an obvious assassination I want.

*Player thoughts:* I’m also checking *vassal opinions* and *levy sizes*. Ireland’s wars are usually won by timing: you dogpile someone when they’re already fighting, or when their levies are down, or when their vassals hate them enough to “accidentally” not show up.

---

The first messenger arrives at dusk, soaked and shivering, and the hall smells briefly of wet wool.

He brings news in the old way: not simply information, but a reminder that the world keeps moving whether a king is ready or not.

A petty king to the west has married his daughter to a man with ambitions. Another has quarreled with his brother and now their household is split like a log. Somewhere north, a chieftain has started calling himself something grander than he deserves.

Murchad listens, and in his mind each fact becomes a stone. He stacks them carefully, building the shape of the future.

When the messenger is dismissed, Murchad’s wife—quiet until now—speaks with the gentle directness of someone who has learned that men in power need to be guided the way horses are guided: firm hands, no shouting.

“Do you intend to be King of Ireland,” she asks, “or merely King of Leinster?”

The hall goes still. Even the fire seems to listen.

Murchad looks at her. He thinks of the old High Kings, the songs that make them larger than life, and the graves that make them smaller than memory.

“I intend to live,” he says.

It is not a coward’s answer. It is a ruler’s. In Ireland, survival is the first crown.

*Player thoughts:* Marriage and alliances time. Ireland’s early marriages aren’t about romance; they’re about **non-aggression pacts, claims, and inheritable traits.** I’m scanning courts for:  
- strong alliances (Wales/Scotland can be handy),  
- claimants to nearby counties,  
- good genetics (quick, strong, genius) if I can afford the prestige hit.  

*Player thoughts:* Also: **heirs.** I want a clean succession. CK2 is less “paint the map” and more “keep your dynasty from exploding.” If I get gavelkind (likely early), I need either enough land to distribute without losing my core, or I need to work toward a better succession law later. For now, I’m planning with the assumption that my first ruler is a foundation, not a finisher.

---

The chaplain requests a private audience before night’s end.

In the small chapel, candles lean in their own wax. The air is close with incense and damp stone. The chaplain’s voice is low, like confession turned outward.

“The Church sees everything,” he says, which is a comfort and a threat depending on the day. “And the Church remembers who provides.”

Murchad studies the altar. Christ’s face carved in wood looks less like peace than endurance.

“Are you asking for alms,” Murchad says, “or offering forgiveness?”

The chaplain smiles. “Both, if Your Grace is wise.”

Murchad knows this language. Ireland is full of it—men speaking around the point as if bluntness might anger the spirits. The chaplain is telling him something simple: piety is currency, and the Pope is a bank that sometimes lends.

Murchad leaves the chapel with the faint feeling that he has been measured.

---

*Player thoughts:* I’m watching my **gold** like a hawk. Early buildings are slow returns, but sitting on cash is also wasted momentum. I’m considering:  
- saving for mercenaries if a surprise war hits,  
- upgrading a holding (usually economy first),  
- keeping enough to bribe a dangerous vassal if needed.  

*Player thoughts:* And yes, I’m eyeing the Pope fund. But I need decent piety and good relations. No point begging if he’s going to laugh and send me a stern letter instead of gold.

---

Weeks pass, and the rhythm of rule reveals itself: petitions, disputes over grazing land, priests arguing about rights, nobles insisting their insults are matters of state.

Then comes the sort of opportunity that does not knock politely.

A neighbor’s council fractures. A minor lord refuses to answer a call to arms. Rumors of sickness in a rival’s household creep through taverns like smoke.

Murchad hears all of it through his spymaster, a man who never brings a report without also bringing a suggestion.

“We could press them now,” the spymaster says softly. “A short war. A clean one. One county. A single bite.”

Murchad does not answer immediately.

He stands again at the window slit, watching Leinster’s fields under a weak sun. Men down there are specks, moving like thoughts across the land. He wonders, briefly, how many of them would die because two lords decided the map should look different.

A king is a man who can do violence at a distance and still sleep. Or he is a man who learns to.

“I will not be the first to bleed,” Murchad says, though it sounds like he is bargaining with fate rather than issuing policy.

The spymaster inclines his head, patient. “Then we arrange it so they bleed first.”

---

*Player thoughts:* Here’s the real moment: I’ve got the usual early-game itch to expand, but I’m forcing myself to slow down. Rushing a war in Ireland can go wrong if:  
- I misjudge levy totals,  
- an ally joins unexpectedly,  
- I get raided mid-war,  
- or I trigger a chain of opportunistic declarations.

*Player thoughts:* So instead of declaring immediately, I’m setting up:  
1) **Fabricate claim** and wait for it to fire (and hope the cost isn’t brutal).  
2) **Marry for an alliance** so I can swing a war safely.  
3) Keep levies unraised until the last moment to preserve morale and avoid opinion penalties.  
4) Watch my neighbors’ wars—best time to strike is when their troops are already dead somewhere else.

---

On the first night of spring, Murchad dreams of a crown that does not fit his head. It sits too heavy, pressing his brow until he wakes with his teeth clenched.

He rises before dawn and walks the keep, hearing only his own footsteps and the distant restlessness of animals. The castle feels less like a home than a ship—wood and stone keeping the dark water out.

In the yard, a few soldiers practice with blunted spears, their breath white in the cold air. The marshal barks corrections like prayers.

Murchad watches them and feels an unfamiliar clarity settle in him.

If Leinster is to be safe, it cannot be small.

Small things are eaten.

He returns to his council chamber and lays a hand on the map—parchment mottled with age, borders drawn by men who would swear they were eternal and then die the next winter.

“One county,” he says, almost to himself. “Then another. Carefully. Quietly.”

The steward exhales as if he has been holding his breath for months. The marshal’s eyes brighten. The chaplain looks resigned. The spymaster smiles like a man watching a door finally open.

Murchad feels, for the first time, the shape of his reign: not a blaze, but a slow gathering.

Outside, rain begins again—thin at first, then steady, drumming on the roof like impatient fingers.

Ireland, as always, keeps its own counsel.
