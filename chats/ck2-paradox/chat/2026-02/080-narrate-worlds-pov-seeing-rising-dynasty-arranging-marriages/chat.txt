I have watched empires rise and fall since the first stone was laid upon another. I understand the logic of kings. I understand that a marriage is a transaction: a daughter for a border fort, a son for a trade route, a cousin to ensure the grain keeps flowing from the south.

But then, *They* rose.

At first, I thought them just another ambitious house, clawing their way up from a single county in the rain-soaked mud of Ireland or the scorching sands of Spain. They played the game as I have always known it. They married the daughters of Dukes to become Dukes. They married the sisters of Kings to claim claims. This was the rhythm of history, a song I knew well.

Then, the rhythm broke. The obsession began.

They stopped looking at land. They stopped looking at gold. They began to look... *inside*.

I felt their gaze sweeping across my surface, a terrifying, omniscience that no mortal should possess. It was as if they had stripped the skin from the world to look at the veins beneath. They could see the invisible threads of history that I thought were lost to time.

They sent a grand embassy to the court of the Byzantine Emperor. The world nodded; this made sense. The Purple Born are prestigious. But the proposal was not for the Princess, nor the influential niece.

They demanded the hand of a scullery maid.

The court gasped. The Emperor laughed. But the Dynasty did not laugh. They paid a king’s ransom in gold for a woman with no shoes, a woman whose father was a forgotten courtier, whose grandfather was a bastard. Why?

Because the Dynasty saw what I had forgotten: that eight generations ago, a Saint had walked her bloodline. They saw the tiny, shimmering icon of holy ancestry hovering over her head, invisible to the naked eye, but blazing like a beacon to *Them*. They did not want the woman; they wanted the drop of saintly blood she carried, to pour it into their own chalice.

It became a madness.

From the frozen steppes of Tartaria, they plucked a hunchbacked woman of the Ashina clan. She was ugly, cruel, and poor. But she carried the blood of the Great Wolf. The Dynasty married her to their heir, a golden youth who could have had the pick of Europe. The lords of the realm whispered of witchcraft. They called it madness. I saw it for what it was: collection.

They sent ships to the burning heat of Abyssinia, seeking the Solomonids. They ignored religion. They ignored culture. They ignored the fact that the bride spoke a tongue the groom would never understand. They only cared that she carried the legacy of the Queen of Sheba.

They breached the impossible distance to India, arranging a matrilineal betrothal with a minor Rajah’s third son. Why? Because he was the last vessel of a conqueror who had died three hundred years prior.

It was a industrialization of biology.

To the rest of the world, their family tree looked like a disaster—a tangle of conflicting cultures, religions, and ethnicities. But when *They* looked at it—through that terrifying, glowing interface that hangs above reality—they saw a masterpiece.

They were weaving a genetic tapestry.

They hunted the blood of Charlemagne (the Karling stain, they called it, yet they coveted it). They hunted the blood of Genghis. They hunted the blood of Alexander, miraculously discovered in a hedge knight.

And then, the generations turned.

The children of these bizarre unions began to be born. And I, the World, felt a chill run through my continents.

The heir was no longer just a man. He was a repository of history.

He stood upon the balcony, and I looked at him. He possessed the genius intellect of a grandmother plucked from a Greek island. He possessed the Herculean strength of a grandfather bought from a Nordic tribe. He was beautiful, a trait bred into him like one breeds horses.

But more than that, his blood screamed.

When he spoke, the authority of the Roman Caesars, the Persian Shahanshahs, the Mongol Khans, and the Catholic Saints resonated in his voice. His character sheet—that floating parchment only his master could see—was so long it trailed off the edge of reality. He was "The Sayyid," he was "The Blood of the Dragon," he was "The descendant of the Perfect Knight."

The other kings of the world looked at him and felt an instinctual, primal fear. They saw a man. I saw a construct.

The Dynasty had won. They had not just conquered land; they had conquered history itself, compressing thousands of years of legendary figures into a single, terrifying bloodline.
