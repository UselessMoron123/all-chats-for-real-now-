I have watched a thousand dynasties rise on the backs of swords, and a thousand more collapse because a son caught a fever or a brother wanted the chair more than the crown. Conquest is loud. It shakes my roads and fills my rivers. Marriage is quieter. It is ink, wax, and the slow tightening of a net.

The house you guide does not conquer the old way, not first. It *collects*.

At the beginning they are only a name—one more banner in the clutter of my fields. Their lands are modest, their chapel cold in winter, their vaults thin. Yet I can feel an attention on them, a pressure like a fingertip on the page of my ledger. While other lords stare at borders, your line stares at *blood*.

Not blood spilled, but blood remembered.

You send envoys with honeyed tongues and hands full of gifts. You host hunts and feasts that aren’t really about venison. You ask questions that make courtiers blink: *Who was her grandmother? Was that duke’s mother of the sainted line? Is that boy truly legitimate?*

My bishops mutter about pride. My old nobles laugh—until the laughter thins, because they begin to notice a pattern: every generation, your family’s marriages look less like romance and more like accounting.

Sometimes it is easy, almost vulgar in its simplicity.

A neighboring realm is prospering—flush with coin, crowned with victories, draped in the glow of a famous ancestor. Their bloodline is celebrated in songs and written in bright pigment in monastery margins. You do not even need the main branch. A third son will do. A cousin in a pleasant county. A spare daughter whose dowry is not land but *heritage*. You take the match when the door is open, while others are still debating etiquette, and you fasten another name to your own.

I feel the web strengthen.

The world approves of those unions. They look normal from the outside: highborn meets highborn, alliances made, children promised. The heralds can explain it in a single sentence. The priests can bless it without choking.

But there are other times—more often, as the easy prizes are taken—when your hunger for lineage becomes a scavenger’s craft.

Then I see it: the long winter evenings in a candlelit solar, where the ruler’s “interest in history” is not scholarly but predatory. They unroll genealogies like maps of buried treasure. *Here.*  
A cadet of a cadet of a cadet, seven degrees removed from any throne worth coveting. A count’s nephew’s widow’s only child. A woman with a claim no one fears and a bloodline no one remembers unless they are looking for it on purpose.

You are looking on purpose.

Because bloodlines are not just prizes. They are *people*, and people die.

*Their children will not be his father’s dynasty.*  
*The husband will be swallowed.*

Some call it an insult. Some call it witchcraft. Many call it humiliating. The prospective groom’s family howls. His father refuses. His brothers threaten violence. Your diplomats return with bruised pride and fresh demands.

And still you push, because the ledger in your mind is clearer than the outrage in their throats.

When you succeed, the world shudders in small ways. A priest hesitates before the blessing. A nobleman spits afterward, as if to rid his mouth of the taste. A vassal uses the moment to question your “proper order.” A rival writes a nasty letter about degeneracy and stolen names. The court whispers: *They marry like spiders.*

History is not fair, and it is rarely convenient.
